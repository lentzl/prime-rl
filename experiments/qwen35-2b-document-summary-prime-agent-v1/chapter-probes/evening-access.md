The Canal Reading Room tried opening later on Tuesdays and Thursdays for twelve weeks. Its normal forty-hour week became a forty-eight-hour week, with four additional hours on each evening. The purpose was to find out whether people who could not attend during ordinary hours would use the room, and what a continuing service would require. This chapter reviews the trial and the limited recommendation that followed it. The recommendation is not an announcement of a permanent change.

The entrance counter recorded an average of 400 visits a week during the six weeks immediately before the trial and 456 during the trial. Weekly visits therefore rose by fourteen per cent. However, the room was open for twenty per cent more hours. Visits per open hour fell from ten to nine and a half. Both descriptions are true: the room accommodated more visits overall, but the average rate during opening hours was lower. Neither measure alone answers whether evening access was worthwhile.

These counts describe entries, not distinct people. Someone who visited on three evenings contributed three visits, and the counter could not tell whether an evening visitor had previously attended during the day. The additional 56 weekly visits must not be described as 56 newly reached residents. Nor does the comparison establish that the extended hours alone caused the increase. The trial coincided with an examination period and a new adult education class nearby. There was no comparison site with unchanged hours, and the six-week baseline did not cover the same part of the year.

Visitors left 73 voluntary feedback slips. Forty-six favored keeping some evening opening and twenty-seven preferred spending the available money on daytime services. Several evening respondents mentioned studying after work or attending after someone else could take over caring responsibilities. These comments explain why later access may matter even when the average hourly count is lower. They are not a representative survey: people who did not visit had no opportunity to leave a slip, frequent visitors could submit more than one, and staff did not record how many people declined to respond.

The trial required 96 additional paid staff hours. A temporary grant covered the resulting 1,920 in staffing costs, and ordinary daytime hours were not reduced to fund it. The calculation did not include a separate estimate for lighting, heating or building maintenance. Volunteers sometimes helped visitors find materials, but they did not replace the paid supervisor. Calling the trial free because a grant paid for it would confuse the source of the money with the cost of providing the service.

That grant ended with the trial. The reading room had no committed replacement funding when the report was written. The fact that all twelve weeks were delivered within the temporary staffing allocation therefore does not show that a permanent extension would fit the ordinary budget. Equally, the report does not conclude that evening opening is unaffordable. It identifies a financing question that has not yet been answered. The operating team was asked to price the full service, including costs omitted from the trial calculation, before any permanent decision.

Access to the building also differed after seven in the evening. The step-free front entrance closed when the shared reception desk closed, leaving a side entrance reached by three steps. A portable ramp was occasionally available, but its availability was not recorded and could not be assumed. Two respondents specifically reported being unable to use the side entrance. These reports do not measure how many other people stayed away. They do show why an extra opening hour is not necessarily an extra accessible hour for everyone.

Keeping the front entrance open later would require an agreement with the building operator and a cost estimate. Neither had been secured by the end of the report. Staff therefore could not describe the access problem as repaired merely because they had identified a possible remedy. Better publicity might explain which entrance was available, but publicity by itself would not remove the steps or guarantee the ramp. The physical and operating arrangements needed their own decision.

The review group recommended considering a further twelve-week test on one evening a week, rather than immediately retaining both evenings or making a permanent extension. That test was conditional on replacement funding and a reliable step-free entrance being arranged first. The group proposed recording attendance by time band, inviting feedback from non-users as well as visitors, and separating repeated visits from newly reached users where this could be done without collecting unnecessary personal information. The recommendation had not yet been approved or implemented.

The narrower proposed test would not simply reproduce half of the first trial's results. Attendance on one evening might change when the other evening was unavailable, and the examination period would not necessarily recur. The next decision should therefore consider who gains usable access, what the full ongoing cost would be, and how strong the evidence is, rather than treating either the higher weekly count or the lower hourly count as a verdict. The trial demonstrated that evening opening could be delivered under temporary arrangements; it did not settle which continuing arrangement should be adopted.
